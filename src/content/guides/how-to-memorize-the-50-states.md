Knowing where the 50 states are is one of those skills everyone assumes they have until they face a blank map. The good news is that the map has a structure, and once you see it, memorizing the states stops being a list of 50 names and becomes four small puzzles.

This guide walks through a method that works for students studying for a geography test and for adults who simply want to stop mixing up Iowa and Idaho. Each step ends with a quiz you can take on this site.

## Step 1: Split the map into four regions

The US Census Bureau groups the states into four regions. They're a convenient way to break the map into pieces of manageable size:

Nine, twelve, sixteen and thirteen are all numbers you can hold in your head in one sitting. Fifty is not.

## Step 2: Start each region with its anchors

An anchor is a state you already know, or one whose shape is impossible to forget. Place it first, then fit its neighbors around it.

- **West:** California and its long coast, then Nevada inland beside it. Wyoming and Colorado are both rectangles: Wyoming is the northern one.
- **South:** Texas, the largest of the lower 48. Florida's peninsula anchors the other end.
- **Midwest:** Michigan, the only state made of two peninsulas, with the Great Lakes around it.
- **Northeast:** New York, the largest state in the region, with New England fanning out to its east.

## Step 3: Use the patterns the map gives you

US borders are full of shortcuts once you notice them:

1. **The Four Corners.** Utah, Colorado, Arizona and New Mexico meet at a single point, the only place in the country where four states touch.
2. **The plains stack.** From north to south, North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska and Kansas sit in a near-perfect column, with Oklahoma and Texas below.
3. **Pairs.** North Dakota is above South Dakota, North Carolina above South Carolina, and West Virginia lies northwest of Virginia, not west of it.
4. **Mirror images.** Vermont and New Hampshire are the classic pair: Vermont is wider at the top, New Hampshire wider at the bottom.
5. **Rivers as borders.** The Mississippi River forms much of the border between the states on either side of it, from Minnesota and Wisconsin down to Louisiana and Mississippi.

Short sessions spread over several days work better than one long session, because each day you retrieve what you learned the day before. That act of remembering is what makes it stick.

## The states people miss most

Some states cost points on almost every first attempt:

- **Rhode Island, Delaware and Connecticut**, simply because they're small. Zoom in on a phone.
- **Iowa and Idaho.** Iowa is in the Midwest between the Mississippi and Missouri rivers; Idaho is in the West, with a panhandle reaching up to Canada.
- **Alabama and Mississippi.** Alabama is the eastern one.
- **Vermont and New Hampshire.** Use the mirror trick above.
- **Alaska and Hawaii**, which most US maps, including ours, show as insets in the lower left. Their real positions are far to the northwest and southwest.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to memorize the 50 states?

With ten to fifteen minutes a day, most people can place all 50 within about a week. Learning one region at a time is much faster than trying the whole map from the start.

### What is the easiest way to learn the states?

Break the map into the four Census regions, start each region with a state you already know, and test yourself on a blank map right after studying. The quiz shows you every state you miss so you know what to review.

### Which states are hardest to find on a map?

The small Northeastern states, especially Rhode Island, Delaware and Connecticut, and look-alike pairs such as Vermont and New Hampshire or Alabama and Mississippi.

### Why are Alaska and Hawaii in the corner of the map?

So the map can show all 50 states at a readable size. Alaska is far larger than it looks there, and both states are shown away from their true positions.
