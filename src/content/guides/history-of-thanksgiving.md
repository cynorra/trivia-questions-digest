Much of what people "know" about the first Thanksgiving comes from 19th-century paintings and school plays, not from the people who were there. Here is what the historical record actually says, and the facts behind our [Thanksgiving trivia](/thanksgiving-trivia/).

## Before the feast

The Mayflower arrived off Cape Cod in November 1620 carrying about 100 passengers. They settled at Plymouth, on land where the Patuxet people had lived until an epidemic a few years earlier. About half of the colonists died during the first winter.

The land was part of the homeland of the **Wampanoag**, a confederation of communities in what is now southeastern Massachusetts and Rhode Island. In March 1621, the Wampanoag leader **Massasoit** (whose personal name was Ousamequin) made a treaty of mutual protection with the colonists.

Two Native men are central to the story:

- **Samoset**, an Abenaki man, walked into the settlement and greeted the colonists in English.
- **Tisquantum (Squanto)**, a Patuxet man who had been kidnapped and taken to Europe years earlier, spoke English and taught the colonists to plant corn.

## The 1621 harvest celebration

Almost everything we know comes from one letter by colonist **Edward Winslow**, written in December 1621. He describes:

- The colonists going out to hunt fowl after the harvest.
- About **90 Wampanoag men** arriving with Massasoit.
- The Wampanoag going out and killing **five deer**, which they gave to the colonists' leaders.
- Eating and "recreations" lasting **three days**.

Winslow doesn't use the word "Thanksgiving," and he doesn't say exactly why the Wampanoag came. Some historians think they may have heard the colonists firing guns in celebration and come to check under the terms of the treaty.

## How it became a national holiday

For two centuries, days of thanksgiving were declared locally and irregularly. George Washington proclaimed a one-time national day of thanksgiving in 1789.

The modern holiday owes most to **Sarah Josepha Hale**, editor of Godey's Lady's Book, who campaigned for 17 years and wrote to five presidents. In **1863**, during the Civil War, **Abraham Lincoln** proclaimed the last Thursday of November a national day of thanksgiving, and presidents have proclaimed it every year since.

In **1939**, Franklin D. Roosevelt moved it a week earlier to lengthen the Christmas shopping season during the Depression, a change critics called "Franksgiving." The confusion led Congress in **1941** to fix Thanksgiving on the **fourth Thursday of November**, where it remains.

## Modern traditions

- **The Macy's parade** began in 1924. Giant balloons first appeared in 1927.
- **Football:** the Detroit Lions have hosted a Thanksgiving game since 1934 (apart from a break during World War II), and the Dallas Cowboys since 1966.
- **The turkey pardon** became a formal annual White House event under George H. W. Bush in 1989.

## Remembering the whole story

Since 1970, some Native people and supporters have gathered at Plymouth on Thanksgiving for a National Day of Mourning, recalling the losses that followed colonization. Telling the 1621 story with the Wampanoag at its center, as the sources do, is both more accurate and more interesting.

## Frequently asked questions

### What really happened at the first Thanksgiving?

In autumn 1621, the Plymouth colonists held a harvest celebration. About 90 Wampanoag men led by Massasoit joined them for three days, and the Wampanoag contributed five deer.

### Did the Pilgrims eat turkey at the first Thanksgiving?

Possibly. Winslow mentions wildfowl, which may have included wild turkey, but the only meat he names specifically is venison.

### When did Thanksgiving become a national holiday?

Abraham Lincoln proclaimed it an annual national holiday in 1863. Congress fixed the fourth Thursday of November in 1941.

### Who was Sarah Josepha Hale?

A magazine editor and writer who campaigned for 17 years for a national Thanksgiving holiday. She also wrote the poem 'Mary Had a Little Lamb'.
