Strong trivia players aren't walking encyclopedias. The same topics come up again and again (presidents, planets, capitals, famous films, Bible stories, sports firsts), so you don't need to know everything, just the things quizzes tend to ask and a way to reason when you're not sure. Ten minutes a day, such as one daily quiz, adds up quickly. Here is a weekly plan first, then the nine habits behind it, grouped into knowing, practicing and playing.

## Know: build the base

**Learn the anchor lists.** A few lists answer a surprising share of questions, and you learn them once for years of payoff: the [US presidents in order](/guides/us-presidents-in-order/), the [planets in order](/guides/planets-in-order/) and their key facts, the major dates of [World War II](/guides/world-war-2-timeline/), the [books of the Bible](/guides/books-of-the-bible-in-order/) if your quizzes include Bible rounds, and the continents, oceans and capitals of the largest countries.

**Know the common categories.** Most quizzes draw on the same dozen subjects: general knowledge, history, science, geography, sports, film and TV, music, food, and holidays. Spend your study time where the questions are. Our category pages, from [general knowledge](/general-knowledge-trivia/) to [music](/music-trivia/), cover each one.

**Learn the myths.** Some questions are built around popular misconceptions, and some quizzes get them wrong. Knowing the common ones, from goldfish memory to Napoleon's height, helps either way. See [15 popular facts that are wrong](/guides/common-trivia-myths/).

## Practice: make it stick

**Practice a little every day.** Short, regular practice beats long cramming. A daily quiz, such as [today's trivia quiz](/daily-trivia/), takes about five minutes and spreads questions across categories. Over a month that's 300 questions, each with an explanation.

**Read the explanation, not just the answer.** When you miss a question, the explanation is where the learning is. "Venus, not Mercury, is the hottest planet because its atmosphere traps heat" teaches you a reason, and reasons stick far better than isolated facts.

**Connect new facts to things you know.** A fact attached to something you already know is easier to recall. Knowing Nixon was president during the Moon landing links two facts at once. Knowing Canberra was built as a compromise between Sydney and Melbourne makes the capital hard to forget.

## Play: at the table

**Use multiple-choice logic.** When you're unsure, eliminate:

- **Remove the impossible.** If the question is about the 1960s, an option from the 1990s is out.
- **Watch for the obvious trap.** Quiz writers often include the answer most people believe, such as Sydney for Australia's capital. If an option seems too obvious, ask why it's there.
- **Prefer the specific.** Between two plausible options, the more precise one is often right.
- **Go with your first instinct** if you have a reason for it; change only when you remember something concrete.

**Keep up with a few areas.** Pop culture and sports change fastest. You don't need to follow everything, but skimming the year's big films, the Super Bowl result and the Oscar winners covers most "recent" questions.

**Build a balanced team.** At a pub quiz, the best team isn't four history buffs. It's a mix: someone for sports, someone for music and film, someone for science, someone for history. Talk through each question briefly and trust the person who knows the area. In most pub quizzes there's no penalty for a wrong answer, so always answer.
