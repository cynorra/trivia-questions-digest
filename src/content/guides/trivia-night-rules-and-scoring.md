Most trivia arguments aren't about knowledge. They're about rules nobody stated: is "Lincoln" enough, or do you need "Abraham Lincoln"? Does a misspelled answer count? Can a team change an answer after the host has read it? Settle these before the first question and the night runs itself.

## The five rules to read out first

1. **No phones or smart watches.** A team caught looking anything up scores zero for that round.
2. **The host's answer is final.** If a team thinks the answer is wrong, they can raise it at the break with a source, and the host decides.
3. **Spelling doesn't matter if the answer is clear.** "Tchaikovski" for Tchaikovsky counts.
4. **Last names are fine for people.** "Armstrong" is enough for the first person on the Moon, unless the question asks for a full name.
5. **The final round is worth double.** This keeps teams who fall behind early in the game.

Write them on a board or the first slide. When a dispute comes up, you can point to them instead of improvising.

### Close answers

Decide your policy and stick to it. A good default: accept the answer if a fair person would know exactly what the team meant. "The Packers" for the Green Bay Packers counts; "Green Bay" probably does too. "Wisconsin" does not.

### Multiple-choice rounds

If you read out four options, as our [category pages](/general-knowledge-trivia/) do, teams can just write A, B, C or D. That makes marking fast and removes spelling disputes completely. For a harder round, drop the options and accept written answers.

### Jokers

A joker lets a team double its score in one round of its choice, declared before the round starts. It rewards teams for knowing their strengths, and it creates a big swing late in the night.

## Tiebreaker questions

A tiebreaker should be a number question that nobody is likely to know exactly. Each team writes a number; closest wins. Some good ones from our question sets:

- How many gifts are given in total in "The Twelve Days of Christmas"? **364**
- How many laps make up the Indianapolis 500? **200**
- How many keys are on a standard piano? **88**
- How many rooms are in the White House? **132**
- In what year was the first Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade? **1924**

Always prepare a second tiebreaker in case two teams are equally close. If they're still tied, ask a question with a sudden-death answer: the first team to write the right answer and hand it in wins.

## Handling disputes

Disputes usually come from an ambiguous question. When a team has a fair point, say so and give the point to everyone who gave the alternative answer. It's better to be generous once than to lose the room. When the team is simply wrong, show them the source; every answer on this site lists one at the bottom of its page.

## Keeping it fair for mixed groups

- Balance teams so experts are spread out.
- Mix rounds: something for history fans, something for film fans, something for families. See [trivia round ideas](/guides/trivia-round-ideas/).
- Give a small prize for the most improved team between the halves.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the basic rules of a pub quiz?

No phones, teams of four to six, one answer per question, sheets handed in at the end of each round, and the quizmaster's answers are final.

### How do you break a tie in trivia?

Ask a number question nobody will know exactly, such as how many gifts are in 'The Twelve Days of Christmas'. The team closest to the right number wins.

### Should spelling count in trivia?

Usually not. If the answer is clearly the right one, accept it. Spelling only matters when the question is about spelling.

### What is a joker round in trivia?

A round that a team chooses in advance to score double. Each team can play its joker once per night.
