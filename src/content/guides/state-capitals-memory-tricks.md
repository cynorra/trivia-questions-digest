State capitals are a memory test with a twist: dozens of them are not the city you'd expect. Albany, not New York City. Springfield, not Chicago. Tallahassee, not Miami. That's what makes a state capitals quiz hard, and it's also what makes the capitals easier to learn once you sort them sensibly.

## Pile 1: the capitals you already know

Start by counting your free points. These capitals are also each state's best-known city, so most people get them without studying:

- Boston, Massachusetts
- Atlanta, Georgia
- Denver, Colorado
- Phoenix, Arizona
- Honolulu, Hawaii
- Nashville, Tennessee
- Salt Lake City, Utah
- Indianapolis, Indiana
- Oklahoma City, Oklahoma

Two of those even carry the state's name. Write down any others you're already sure of; the rest of this guide is about the ones left over.

## Pile 2: the famous-city traps

These are the capitals people get wrong most, because a bigger, better-known city gets in the way. The trick is to learn the pair together: the city you'd guess and the real capital.

Many of these capitals were chosen in the 1800s precisely because they were central or neutral, not because they were the biggest city. Remembering that reason makes the "wrong" answer feel less arbitrary.

## Pile 3: the surprises, with hooks

The capitals left over are mostly smaller cities. A hook (a person, a sound, or a word picture) helps them stick.

- **Named after presidents:** Jefferson City (Missouri), Lincoln (Nebraska), Madison (Wisconsin), Jackson (Mississippi).
- **Named after other people:** Bismarck, North Dakota, after the German chancellor Otto von Bismarck; Carson City, Nevada, after the frontiersman Kit Carson; Pierre, South Dakota, after the fur trader Pierre Chouteau Jr.
- **Sound-alikes:** Juneau, Alaska ("Do you know Juneau?"); Boise, Idaho; Dover, Delaware.
- **Easy-to-swap pairs:** Columbia is South Carolina's capital and Columbus is Ohio's; Charleston is West Virginia's capital, while Charleston, South Carolina, is not a capital at all; Concord, New Hampshire, and Frankfort, Kentucky, both sound like they belong in New England.

Pierre (South Dakota), Montpelier (Vermont) and Frankfort (Kentucky) are among the most often missed, because they are small and rarely in the news. There are 50 state capitals; Washington, DC is the national capital, not a state capital.

## Learn them region by region

Just like the map, capitals are easier in groups. A suggested order:

1. **Northeast (9 states):** Augusta, Concord, Montpelier, Boston, Providence, Hartford, Albany, Trenton, Harrisburg.
2. **West (13):** Olympia, Salem, Sacramento, Carson City, Boise, Helena, Cheyenne, Salt Lake City, Denver, Phoenix, Santa Fe, Juneau, Honolulu.
3. **Midwest (12):** Columbus, Indianapolis, Springfield, Lansing, Madison, Saint Paul, Des Moines, Jefferson City, Bismarck, Pierre, Lincoln, Topeka.
4. **South (16):** Dover, Annapolis, Richmond, Charleston, Raleigh, Columbia, Atlanta, Tallahassee, Frankfort, Nashville, Montgomery, Jackson, Little Rock, Baton Rouge, Oklahoma City, Austin.

In the [state capitals quiz](/state-capitals-quiz/), choose a region from the menu to practice just those states. The wrong answers come from the same region, so you can't get by on knowing which part of the country a city is in.

## A practice routine that works

- Day 1: Northeast. Quiz until you can get all nine.
- Day 2: West, plus one round of the Northeast.
- Day 3: Midwest.
- Day 4: South, the biggest group.
- Day 5: all 50, then only the ones you missed.

Say each pair out loud as one phrase, such as "Montpelier, Vermont." Hearing the state and capital together builds the link faster than reading a list.
