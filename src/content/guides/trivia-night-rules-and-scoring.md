Most trivia arguments aren't about knowledge. They're about rules nobody stated: is "Lincoln" enough, or do you need "Abraham Lincoln"? Does a misspelled answer count? Can a team change an answer after the host has read it? Below is a one-page rules sheet to read out first, then the rulings for the disagreements that come up every time, then scoring and tiebreakers. Settle these before the first question and the night runs itself.

## The sheet to read out

1. **No phones or smart watches.** A team caught looking anything up scores zero for that round.
2. **The host's answer is final.** If a team thinks the answer is wrong, they can raise it at the break with a source, and the host decides.
3. **Spelling doesn't matter if the answer is clear.** "Tchaikovski" for Tchaikovsky counts.
4. **Last names are fine for people.** "Armstrong" is enough for the first person on the Moon, unless the question asks for a full name.
5. **The final round is worth double.** This keeps teams who fall behind early in the game.

Write them on a board or the first slide. When a dispute comes up, you can point instead of improvising.

## Rulings for the arguments that always come up

**"We said 'Green Bay'. Does that count for the Packers?"** Accept the answer if a fair person would know exactly what the team meant. "The Packers" counts, and "Green Bay" probably does too. "Wisconsin" does not. Decide your policy once and apply it to every team.

**"The question was ambiguous."** Usually it was. When a team has a fair point, say so and give the point to everyone who gave the alternative answer. It's better to be generous once than to lose the room.

**"The answer is wrong."** When the team is simply wrong, show them the source; every answer on this site lists one at the bottom of its page.

**"We only got half of it."** Decide in advance. Half-right answers score either 0 or ½, for example ½ for one of two parts.

**"Can we change our answer?"** Not once the host has read the answer or the sheets have been collected. Collecting at the end of each round, not each question, keeps this simple.

If you read out four options, as our [category pages](/trivia/general-knowledge-trivia/) do, teams can just write A, B, C or D. That makes marking fast and removes spelling disputes completely. For a harder round, drop the options and accept written answers.

A **joker** lets a team double its score in one round of its choice, declared before the round starts. It rewards teams for knowing their strengths and creates a big swing late in the night.

## Tiebreakers

A tiebreaker should be a number question that nobody is likely to know exactly. Each team writes a number; closest wins. Some good ones from our question sets:

- How many gifts are given in total in "The Twelve Days of Christmas"? **364**
- How many laps make up the Indianapolis 500? **200**
- How many keys are on a standard piano? **88**
- How many rooms are in the White House? **132**
- In what year was the first Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade? **1924**

Always prepare a second tiebreaker in case two teams are equally close. If they're still tied, ask a sudden-death question: the first team to write the right answer and hand it in wins.

## Keeping it fair for mixed groups

Balance teams so experts are spread out. Mix rounds, with something for history fans, something for film fans and something for families; see [trivia round ideas](/guides/trivia-round-ideas/). Give a small prize for the most improved team between the halves.
