Hosting a trivia night is mostly about rhythm. The questions matter, but what people remember is whether the evening moved: no long silences, no arguments about what the host said, and a close finish. This plan counts down from a week before to the last prize, so you can see what to settle when. You can lift every question you need from our [trivia categories](/trivia/general-knowledge-trivia/).

## A week before: the format

Four decisions drive everything else.

<thead><tr><th>Decision</th><th>What works for most groups</th><th>Why</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Team size</td><td>4 to 6 players</td><td>Small enough that everyone speaks, big enough to cover gaps in knowledge</td></tr>
<tr><td>Rounds</td><td>6 rounds of 10 questions</td><td>About 90 minutes of questions, plus breaks</td></tr>
<tr><td>Time per question</td><td>30 seconds (45 if there are no options)</td><td>Long enough to confer, short enough to keep pace</td></tr>
<tr><td>Answer format</td><td>Paper sheets, one per team per round</td><td>Easy to collect, mark and settle disputes</td></tr>

For a small family gathering, four rounds of eight questions is plenty. For a large bar crowd keep six rounds, but cap teams at six: bigger teams get loud and stop listening.

## Four days before: the rounds

The most common mistake is six rounds of the host's favorite subjects. Mix them:

1. **Warm-up: general knowledge.** Easy questions everyone can answer. Our [general knowledge trivia](/trivia/general-knowledge-trivia/) works well here.
2. **A history or science round.** Try [history trivia](/trivia/history-trivia/) or [science trivia](/trivia/science-trivia/).
3. **Pop culture.** [Movie and TV trivia](/trivia/movie-trivia/) or [music trivia](/trivia/music-trivia/). Add short audio clips for a "name that tune" twist.
4. **A themed round.** Match the season: [Christmas](/trivia/christmas-trivia/), [Thanksgiving](/trivia/thanksgiving-trivia/) or [Halloween](/trivia/halloween-trivia/).
5. **A picture or wildcard round.** Celebrity baby photos, logos, or a "name the year" round.
6. **The hard round.** Finish with our [trivia questions for adults](/trivia/trivia-questions-for-adults/), worth double points to keep everyone in the race.

More formats, from "connections" rounds to sound rounds, are in our list of [trivia round ideas](/guides/trivia-round-ideas/).

## Two days before: read every question aloud once

Every question should have exactly one answer a reasonable person would accept. "What's the biggest country?" is a bad question (by area? by population?). "Which country is the largest by area?" is a good one. If you stumble over a question reading it alone, the room will misunderstand it. If you write your own, our guide on [how to write trivia questions](/guides/how-to-write-trivia-questions/) explains how to choose wrong options and avoid trick questions.

Every category page on this site lists its questions with the answers hidden and an explanation for each, so you can read them straight from your phone or print them.

## The day before: print and prepare

- Questions printed, with answers on a separate sheet
- Answer sheets and pens for every team
- A visible scoreboard (whiteboard or a shared screen)
- A timer
- A tiebreaker question and a backup
- A microphone if more than about 30 people are coming

Decide your scoring now. One point per correct answer, double for the final round, and a rule for close answers (spelling mistakes, last names only) written on the board so no one argues later. Our guide to [trivia night rules and scoring](/guides/trivia-night-rules-and-scoring/) covers jokers, bonus rounds and disputes. Pick the tiebreaker as a number question where the closest answer wins, such as "How many gifts are given in 'The Twelve Days of Christmas'?" (364) or "How many laps make up the Indianapolis 500?" (200).

## On the night: a 7 p.m. start

<tr><td>7:15</td><td>Rounds 1 to 3, about 12 minutes each</td></tr>
<tr><td>7:55</td><td>Break, scores posted</td></tr>

</tbody>

Between those marks:

- **Read each question twice**, then the options, then the question once more. People mishear in loud rooms.
- **Give a time warning** ("ten seconds") before moving on.
- **Collect answer sheets at the end of each round**, not each question. It is faster and stops teams changing answers.
- **Read the answers with a one-line explanation.** It turns "the answer was B" into a moment people talk about.
- **Post the scores between rounds.** A visible leaderboard is the single best way to keep energy up.

## After the last answer: prizes

Prizes don't need to be expensive. A box of chocolates, a homemade trophy or bragging rights on a framed certificate works. A prize for last place keeps the bottom teams laughing instead of leaving.
